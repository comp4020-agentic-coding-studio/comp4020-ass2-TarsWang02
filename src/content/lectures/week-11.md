## Question

You've spent ten weeks measuring jumps you designed, with expectations you
already had going in. What can a measurement taken by someone else, who
didn't design the jump and doesn't know what you expected, tell you that
your own trials can't?

## Prediction (skip if you'd rather just look)

Before running anything: if you hand someone a preset without telling them
what it's "supposed" to feel like, do you expect their first attempt to look
more like your third trial, or your first?

## Experiment

Hand a classmate a preset in the Jump Lab with no explanation beyond the
controls. Watch without commenting, coaching, or answering questions about
what's "supposed" to happen — write down only what you actually observe:
where they hesitated, what they tried that you wouldn't have, whether they
noticed the metrics at all. Compare your notes to what you expected before
they sat down.

## Explanation

A playtest is only useful if it captures what actually happened rather than
what you expected to happen. The moment you coach a player toward the
outcome you wanted, you've replaced their observation with your assumption
— which is a more expensive way of measuring your own bias than just writing
it down. The discipline is the same reproducibility instinct from week 3,
pointed at a harder target: this time the "instrument" is a person, and
people vary in ways a deterministic physics engine never will. Recording
what you saw, not what you predicted, is what makes the observation worth
anything afterward.

<details>
<summary>Why "they had fun" isn't an observation</summary>

"Fun" is a conclusion, not a data point — it doesn't tell a reader what
happened, only how you're interpreting it. An observation is something a
second person watching the same session would also have written down: "they
tried to jump three times before checking the metrics," not "they seemed to
enjoy it."

</details>

## Task

Playtest one preset with a classmate, silently. Write down three concrete
observations (not interpretations) of what they did, then one sentence
interpreting what those observations suggest about the preset. Keep the
observations and the interpretation clearly separated.
