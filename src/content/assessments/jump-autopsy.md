## The brief

> Two jumps, same field, same character. What, precisely, is different?

You don't need your own game to run this comparison — the [Jump Lab](/jump-lab/?a=floaty&b=precise&mode=immediate)
link above loads the two supplied jumps for you: the **Floaty** preset as
Jump A and the **Precise** preset as Jump B, both in immediate mode. That's
the whole point of doing this in the browser rather than asking you to bring
footage: the two jumps you're autopsying are one click away, not something
you have to reconstruct from a video.

A good autopsy doesn't just say Jump A feels floatier. It says how much
floatier, measured in world units and seconds, and it connects that
difference back to a specific number in the settings rather than a vibe.
Two students should be able to run the same two presets, follow your method
section, and land on the same table.

## What you submit

A single document — markdown or PDF, your choice — containing:

1. **Measurement table.** Height, displacement and airtime for at least three
   trials of each jump, plus each jump's average.
2. **Phase annotation.** For each jump, a short description of what's
   happening in each of the four phases (anticipation and landing can be
   qualitative; takeoff and flight should cite your measured numbers).
3. **Method.** The preset, mode, and number of trials for each jump — enough
   detail that a marker could reproduce your table from scratch.
4. **Explanation.** A paragraph on *why* the two jumps differ, in terms of
   which parameter (gravity, launch speed, or both) the Floaty and Precise
   presets set differently, and how that produces the measured gap.

Evaluation weighs accuracy, reproducibility and interpretation — this brief
is not a drawing exercise, and a rougher sketch with a correct, reproducible
table beats a polished one with invented numbers.
